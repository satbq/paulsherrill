Paul M. Sherrill
Music Theorist

I am a music theorist who specializes in mathematical music theory and in European vocal music from the 1700s. Regarding the former, I am interested in using mathematical tools (like linear algebra and the theory of hyperplane arrangements) to describe fundamental musical structures like scales, grooves, and voice leadings. My work on this topic has been published in the Journal of Music Theory, Mathematics and Computation in Music 2026, and the Journal of Mathematics and Music. I have also released a software package, called musicMCT, in the language R, which works as a calculator for the concepts I’ve developed (and also for other important concepts in mathematical music theory, like traditional pitch-class set theory and the music-theoretic applications of the Discrete Fourier Transform).

In the world of vocal music, I study how formulas, convention, and usage contribute to the expressive and dramatic meaning of texted works. My work uses ideas from musical schema theory, the “new Formenlehre”, and music semiotics, and is inspired by ideas from various branches of linguistics. I am interested in Italian opera from around 1720 through 1760, in the serious operas of composers like Vinci, Pergolesi, Galuppi, and Hasse. I also love Mozart’s operas (alongside the vocal works of his contemporaries like Marianna Martines and Domenico Cimarosa), and with Matthew Boyle I have published on J. S. Bach’s St. Matthew Passion. My article “Galant Recitative Schemas,” co-authored with Matthew Boyle, received the David Kraehenbuehl Prize and the Society for Music Theory’s Emerging Scholar Award; and my article “Susanna’s ‘Deh vieni’” received the American Musicological Society’s Roland Jackson Award.

I am currently Assistant Professor of Music Theory at the University of Utah. I teach classes on a wide range of subjects, from core curricular topics like harmony to seminars on analyzing the role of music in religious experience, the works of Claude Debussy, and musical styles (like recitative and hiphop) that blur the lines between speech and song. In 2025, I was a recipient of the University of Utah’s Early Career Teaching Award.

For nearly a decade, I was a moderator of the music theory forum on the social-media platform Reddit, r/musictheory, which has informed a lot of my research and teaching, especially my interest in taking apparently rudimentary topics like musical scales seriously. This, together with teaching online in 2020, motivated me to throw together some Python code for animating music theory concepts in the library Manim, which I am happy to share although it’s quite unpolished.
